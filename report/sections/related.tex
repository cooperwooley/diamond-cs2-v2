\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Diffusion world models.}
DIAMOND~\cite{alonso2024diamond} trains a diffusion model, in the EDM formulation~\cite{karras2022edm}, to predict the next observation from a short history of frames and actions.
It was introduced as a world model for reinforcement learning on Atari and was also applied to Counter-Strike: Global Offensive.
We use its denoiser--upsampler architecture and training code (\cref{sec:method}).
GameNGen~\cite{valevski2025gamengen} simulates Doom with a diffusion model that is conditioned on past frames and actions; its training data was recorded from reinforcement-learning agents, which like our bots provide paired data without human effort.
Oasis~\cite{oasis2024} and MineWorld~\cite{guo2025mineworld} are real-time interactive world models for Minecraft, the latter a visual--action autoregressive transformer.
Genie~\cite{bruce2024genie} learns a playable model from videos \emph{without} action labels by inferring latent actions.
We differ in scale and in where the labels come from: we simulate a game that exposes its state, so the actions are not learned.

\paragraph{Counter-Strike data and behavioural cloning.}
Pearce and Zhu~\cite{pearce2021counterstrike} collected Counter-Strike: Global Offensive deathmatch frames together with human keyboard and mouse input and trained an agent by behavioural cloning.
Their action encoding (keys, clicks, and binned mouse deltas) is the 51-dimensional layout that DIAMOND's CS:GO model and our v2 model use.
Our data differs in two ways: it is for CS2, and the actions are reconstructed from state because the demo format gives no reliable input stream (\cref{sec:data}).

\paragraph{Inverse dynamics and action labelling.}
When videos have no action labels, a common approach is to learn them.
VPT~\cite{baker2022vpt} trains an inverse-dynamics model (IDM) on a small labelled set and uses it to label a large corpus of Minecraft videos; Genie~\cite{bruce2024genie} learns latent actions without any labels.
Our labels are instead computed by deterministic rules from game state, which is possible only because the data is generated in the engine.
The rules make every label auditable, at the cost of the systematic errors that we measure and list in \cref{sec:limits}.
A learned IDM is a natural complement and is part of our future work.
